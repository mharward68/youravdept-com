By the time most planners send an AV request for proposal, the venue is signed, the agenda is drafted, and the room block is set. Those three documents have already decided a large share of what AV will cost and how well it can work.

Not because anyone made a bad call, but because nobody was thinking about load-in paths, exclusivity clauses, or 24-hour holds at the moment those calls got made.

You cannot get AV involved too early. That is not a sales line, it is a sequencing fact.

Your AV partner is the only vendor whose cost is driven almost entirely by decisions other people make: which building you pick, how the rooms are held, when the doors open, and how fast the flip has to happen. Bring them in while those are still questions and they can help you shape the answers. Bring them in afterward and all they can do is price what you already committed to.

What's Inside

The four stages of an event

Use it as a checklist for your own process, or hand it to a partner you trust. Either way these items get decided. The only question is whether anyone decided them on purpose.

01

Site selection Where it's decided

Your venue contract is an AV document. It just isn't written like one. Exclusivity, in-house fees, dock and freight paths, load-in rules, and room capacity once the staging is in: the things that are invisible on a floor plan and expensive to discover in month ten.

02

Planning

Your agenda is a budget document. The production schedule, call cadence, rehearsals, crew meal break rules, contingency plans, and technical riders, and how your schedule quietly sets your crew size.

03

Execution

If planning went well, onsite is confirmation rather than correction. The one thing your AV team needs that only you have, and how to keep additions from surprising you on the final invoice.

04

Review

The cheapest hour you will spend on next year's event. How to run a debrief that produces something useful, and why it is also the best moment to negotiate.

A Sample From The Guide

When you should take possession of the room

The item planners most often leave to chance. Setting a room the same morning it goes live is the most expensive way to set a room, and the most likely way to start a conference with a technical problem.

RoomTake possession
General sessionA full day before doors, sometimes more
Breakout roomsThe afternoon or evening before
Meal and reception roomsBefore the flip, not during it
Every contracted room24-hour hold, start to finish

Build that time into the RFP and the contract, not into your hopes for the week.

Without a 24-hour hold, you can find yourself striking a room for a late-night gala and rebuilding it before breakfast. The time you negotiate before you sign is the cheapest labor you will ever buy.

Five questions to ask before you sign a venue contract

Ask the venue. Then have your AV partner read the answers.

1

Which spaces, if any, are exclusive to the in-house AV or rigging vendor?

2

If I bring in an outside AV partner, what will it cost me in fees, all in?

3

When do I take possession of each room, and is it on a 24-hour hold?

4

What does load-in look like from the dock to the meeting space?

5

Do these capacity numbers account for staging, screens, and front of house?

Questions 3, 4 and 5 are in the guide Send it to me

Any venue worth booking will answer all five. Keep the answers: they are the first half of your AV request for proposal.
